Pipe Rerouting vs Jackhammering: Which Fix Is Right for You

Once a slab leak is located, you face a real choice: open the concrete and fix the pipe where it lies, or abandon that section and run a new line overhead. Both are legitimate repairs. They differ in cost, disruption, and what happens the next time a pipe fails.

This blog lays out the honest comparison we give Land O'Lakes homeowners on site, so you can make the call with clear eyes.

Breaking the Slab: Fast and Targeted

Jackhammering means opening the concrete directly above the leak, usually a square about two feet across. We cut out the failed pipe section, install the replacement, pressure test it, and patch the concrete. For a first leak in an otherwise healthy system, this is the fastest and least expensive fix.

The trade-off is what stays behind. The rest of that buried line is the same age and material as the piece that failed. If the system is old, you may be opening concrete again in a year or two for the next weak spot.

Rerouting: No Concrete, No Repeat

A reroute runs a brand new line from the water heater or manifold to the affected fixtures through walls, ceilings, or the attic. The leaking under-slab section is capped at both ends and abandoned. Nothing gets jackhammered, and your floors stay exactly as they are.

The new line is accessible for the rest of its life. If anything ever needs service, it happens in a wall or attic, never under concrete again. For homes with aging systems, this ends the leak cycle instead of resetting it.

  • Zero concrete demolition
  • Floors, tile, and finishes untouched
  • New line accessible for future service
  • Ends repeat leaks on the rerouted branch

Comparing Cost Honestly

A spot repair through the slab costs less today than a reroute. That is the whole appeal, and for a young system with a single failure it is usually the right call. The math changes with the second leak: two spot repairs, two concrete patches, two rounds of disruption, and you still own the same aging line.

We quote both options in writing on every job where both make sense. Most homeowners with one leak choose the spot repair. Most with two choose the reroute. Either way, the decision is yours with real numbers in front of you.

Which Should You Choose

Choose the slab opening when the leak is a first-timer, the rest of the system tests healthy, and the home is under twenty years old. Choose the reroute when leaks are repeating, the piping is galvanized or decades-old copper, or you simply want the concrete left alone.

There is no wrong answer between two honest options, only the one that fits your home and your tolerance for disruption. Ask us to show you the condition of your pipes and quote both paths. Then decide.

What About Epoxy Pipe Lining?

You may hear about epoxy lining, where a resin coating is applied inside the existing pipe to seal it from within. It has real uses, mainly in drain and sewer lines, but for pressurized under-slab supply lines it is a niche option with strict requirements: the host pipe must be sound enough to hold the liner, and the result is only as good as the preparation.

For most Lutz slab leaks, lining is not the right tool. A failed copper line under a slab usually needs replacement or abandonment, not a coating. If a contractor proposes lining, ask for specifics: which pipe, what condition it is in, and what the warranty covers. Then compare that honestly against a reroute before deciding.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is rerouting always more expensive?

Upfront, usually yes. Over the life of an aging system, it is often cheaper than multiple spot repairs.

Does a reroute affect water pressure?

Typically it improves it. New smooth pipe flows better than corroded old pipe, and we can upsize undersized lines.

How long does a reroute take?

Most single-branch reroutes finish in a day. Whole-home repipes take two to four days.

Will I see the new pipes?

No. They run inside walls, ceilings, and attics, insulated and secured out of sight.

Can you reroute in a two-story home?

Yes. We plan the path per home, and two-story reroutes are routine work for us.
