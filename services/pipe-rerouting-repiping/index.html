Pipe rerouting means abandoning a leaking water line under your concrete slab and running a brand new line to the same fixtures through walls, ceilings, or the attic. The old pipe stays buried and capped. Your home gets a new, accessible water line that can be inspected and serviced without ever touching concrete again.

Repiping goes further: replacing some or all of a home's water lines, usually because the original piping is failing in multiple places. In Lutz, that often means original copper worn thin by hard water or galvanized pipe corroding from the inside out. Both rerouting and repiping solve the root problem instead of patching its symptoms.

What Pipe Rerouting Means

A reroute starts at your water heater or main manifold and runs new piping to each affected fixture along a path above the slab. We fish lines through wall cavities and attic spaces, insulate them properly, and connect them with clean, accessible fittings. The leaking under-slab section is disconnected at both ends and left in place.

Because the new line is accessible, any future issue can be fixed without concrete work. Reroutes also let us upsize undersized lines and eliminate the maze of buried fittings that caused the original failure. Most single-fixture reroutes finish in a day.

  • New line run through walls or attic, never under the slab
  • Old leaking section capped and abandoned in place
  • Future repairs need no concrete work
  • Opportunity to upsize undersized original lines
  • Insulated properly for attic temperature swings

Reroute vs Breaking the Slab

Breaking the slab means opening concrete directly above the leak, repairing that one section, and patching it closed. It is the right choice for a first leak in an otherwise healthy system: fast, targeted, and the least expensive option. But it leaves the rest of the aging line in the ground, waiting for its turn.

A reroute costs more upfront than a single spot repair, but it ends the cycle. If your home has had two leaks, or the piping is decades old, rerouting usually costs less than the second and third spot repairs combined, with none of the concrete dust. We will give you honest numbers for both so you can compare.

  • Spot repair: cheapest today, best for a first leak
  • Reroute: no concrete work, ends repeat leaks on that line
  • Two or more leaks usually tips the math toward rerouting
  • Reroutes keep your floors and finishes intact
  • We quote both options so you can decide with real numbers

Materials: Copper vs PEX

Copper is the traditional choice: rigid, proven, and familiar to every plumber. It handles heat well and lasts decades in good water conditions. In Lutz, though, hard water and warm soil can pit copper from the outside, which is why so many original copper lines under slabs eventually fail.

PEX is flexible plastic tubing that has become the standard for reroutes and repipes. It snakes through walls with fewer fittings, resists scale buildup and corrosion, and handles freezing better than rigid pipe. For most reroutes we recommend PEX for its durability and clean installation, but we install copper when a homeowner prefers it.

  • Copper: rigid, proven, familiar, handles heat well
  • PEX: flexible, fewer fittings, resists corrosion and scale
  • PEX is usually the better pick for attic reroutes
  • Both materials meet Florida plumbing code
  • We explain the trade-offs and let you choose

Frequently Asked Questions

About pipe rerouting and repiping in the Lutz area.

Will a reroute change my water pressure?

Usually for the better. We can upsize lines that were undersized originally, and new smooth pipe flows better than corroded old pipe.

Where does the new pipe run?

Through wall cavities, ceilings, and attic spaces, insulated and secured. You will not see it in finished rooms.

Does rerouting require a permit?

Plumbing modifications typically require a permit and inspection in Hillsborough County. We handle the permit paperwork for our jobs.

How long does a whole-home repipe take?

Most whole-home repipes take two to four days depending on the size of the home and the number of fixtures. Water is restored each evening.

What happens to the old pipes?

They are disconnected, drained, capped at both ends, and left buried. Removing them would mean destroying the slab for no benefit.

Is PEX safe for drinking water?

Yes. PEX used for potable water is certified to national drinking water standards, and it is now the most common repipe material in the country.
